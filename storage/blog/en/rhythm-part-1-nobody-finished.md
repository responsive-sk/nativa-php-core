You opened this article. You're still here. That's already unusual.

Most technical articles lose their readers in the first three sentences. Not because the content is bad. Because the rhythm is wrong.

## The problem nobody talks about

Search for "how to implement X in PHP." You'll find thousands of articles. Most start the same way:

"In this article, we will explore the implementation of X in PHP. We will cover the basics, advanced topics, and best practices."

You already left. Not because you're impatient, but because the article told you nothing. It described what it will describe. A table of contents pretending to be an introduction.

The technical content in those articles is often excellent. The code is correct. The explanations are accurate. But the reader is gone before they reach the first code block.

## Why rhythm matters more than content

A well-written article with bad code is still readable. A badly-written article with great code is abandoned.

Rhythm is the pulse of a story, the alternation between tension and release, confusion and clarity, question and answer. Without it, even the best technical content becomes a wall of text.

Think of it this way: a recipe that says "combine ingredients, bake for 30 minutes" is technically correct. A recipe that says "the dough should feel like a baby's ear, soft, warm, slightly tacky" is one you'll remember.

## The seven phases

Every article follows a natural arc:

```
1. INTRO    → Hook. Enter the world.
2. HMLA     → Mystery. The problem.
3. SLNKO    → Clarity. The solution.
4. NOC      → Depth. Why it must be this way.
5. OBJEKT   → Abandoned concept. Respect for the topic.
6. TÉMA     → Core. One human sentence.
7. OUTRO    → Invitation. Not navigation.
```

Most articles have phases 2 and 3. That's it. Problem, solution, done. The reader learns something but forgets it by tomorrow.

The missing phases, INTRO, NOC, OBJEKT, TÉMA, OUTRO, are what make articles memorable. They're also the hardest to write.

## The hook that works

The INTRO is not "In this article we will..." It's a story that starts before the reader has time to think "what is this about?"

```markdown
# Bad: formal intro
In this article, we will discuss the implementation of
content security policy in PHP applications.

# Good: hook
Click a button. Nothing happens. No error, no console output,
no request. The button is visible, styled correctly, hover works.
Click ... silence.
```

The first example tells you what the article is about. The second makes you want to know what happened. That's the difference between a table of contents and a hook.
